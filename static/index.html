The rules it follows

Traffic lights run a 46 s cycle: 18 s green, 3 s amber and 2 s all-red for each direction. On amber the car stops if it can do so braking under 3.2 m/s², otherwise it carries on through. Left turns on green wait for a gap in oncoming traffic.
STOP
Stop signs need a full stop of at least one second at the line. At an all-way stop cars go in the order they arrived. At a two-way stop the side street waits for a safe gap in cross traffic.
Yield signs on Wellington mean slowing down and only entering when cross traffic leaves a gap of about 4 seconds.
MAX40
Speed limits are 40 km/h on most streets, 50 on Front, Spadina and University, and 30 on John and York. The car slows down before it reaches a lower limit, not after.
🚶
Pedestrians at traffic lights only start crossing when the walk phase has at least 9 s left. Jaywalkers in the hazard tests still ignore all of this, which is the point.
The layout and speed limits are read from data/toronto_downtown.osm by Python with BeautifulSoup, so you can change any intersection there. The rule logic lives in static/js/13-traffic.js.

📘 How It Works
The maths behind each part of the prototype.
How the model works

Perception. Every agent within 60 m becomes a track with a class, a 2D box and a confidence score. Confidence falls with range (c = c₀ − 0.28·(d/60)^1.6) plus a little sensor noise.

Prediction. Vehicles use constant acceleration (clamped at a stop). Pedestrians and animals use constant velocity with positional uncertainty that grows over time, σ(t) = σ₀ + k·t. k depends on the class and on whether the object is in the road: a deer in the road gets far more spread than a pedestrian on the sidewalk.

Collision probability. The ego car and other vehicles are capsules. For each candidate plan, the 4-second future is checked every 0.1 s. The closest-approach gap d becomes p = exp(−d²/2σ²), discounted for time. A small proximity term adds risk for anything currently very close.

Decision. Six maneuvers are rolled out and scored with cost = 160·Rmax + 25·ΣR + comfort + progress. The cheapest wins. Hysteresis prevents dithering, but a risky current plan is always replaced immediately. Measured decision latency is shown live.

Routing. The street grid is a graph. Dijkstra searches over (intersection, arrival heading) states, so it never plans a U-turn, and it adds a small cost for right turns and a larger one for left turns. The route becomes a lane-centred path with curved turns, resampled every 0.5 m.

Pure pursuit. The car is a kinematic bicycle model (wheelbase 2.8 m). Each tick it finds a point on the path one lookahead distance Ld ahead of the rear axle. It then steers along the arc that passes through that point: κ = 2·y/Ld², δ = atan(L·κ), with a limit on how fast the wheel can turn. A "nudge" from the planner shifts the path sideways, and pure pursuit follows the shifted path.

Odometry. Dead reckoning adds up wheel-encoder speed (with a 1–1.5% scale error and noise) and gyro yaw rate (with bias and noise), so the raw estimate drifts. Once a second a map-matched position fix (σ ≈ 0.25 m) pulls the fused estimate back with gain 0.55. That's a simple complementary filter, standing in for the EKF a production stack would use.

Ride-hailing. A request carries the phone's GPS fix (σ ≈ 8 m, so it's often on the wrong side of the street). The taxi routes to the curb that GPS suggests. Once within radio range, every BLE signal reading (path-loss exponent 2.3, 3.5 dB noise) and UWB angle reading reweights a particle filter that only allows sidewalk positions. When the estimate shifts along the block, or flips to the other side of the street, the taxi re-plans its stop point.

Traffic rules. Each intersection is a traffic light, an all-way stop, a two-way stop or a yield. Every car runs the same rule engine: it looks up the control for its approach, works out the distance from its front bumper to the stop line, and either holds there or proceeds. Holding works by placing a virtual stopped car on the stop line, so the normal car-following model brakes for it smoothly. The robotaxi turns the same decision into a speed limit on its acceleration, and each point on its route carries that street's speed limit.

PID + Ziegler–Nichols. The planner's acceleration request is integrated into a reference speed. A PID with model-based feed-forward converts it into throttle or brake. Gains come from the Ziegler–Nichols ultimate gain and period of the plant linearized at the current coordinate, with your choice of tuning rule.

Python back-end. Flask serves this page and the API. BeautifulSoup reads the Toronto map XML: street names, block styles, landmarks, streetcar routes and the speed limit. NumPy computes the Ziegler–Nichols profile for each new route. pandas logs and summarises the run. scikit-learn fits the radio path-loss model and the trip-time model, and DBSCAN finds risk hotspots. matplotlib builds the downloadable report. The 60 fps simulation and the drawing stay in JavaScript, because a round-trip to Python every frame would make it laggy. If the page is opened without the server it still runs, in offline mode.
